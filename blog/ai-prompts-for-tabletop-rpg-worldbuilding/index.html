AI Prompts for Tabletop RPG Worldbuilding and Game Prep

Published 2026-10-15 · 3 min read · by AgentKit Co

Most game masters don't struggle with ideas. They struggle with time. You have an hour before the session, three players who just went somewhere you didn't plan, and a town with no name. An AI assistant can be a decent brainstorming partner for exactly this, as long as you keep it in its lane: it offers options, and you make the creative calls.

Below are five prompts that work for any system (or none), plus the habits that make the answers usable at a table. Replace the [brackets] and paste into any chat assistant.

Before you start: some players dislike AI in their hobby, and that's a fair preference. Mention it in session zero. Everything here is for prep and brainstorming. You still run the game.

Give the AI a table brief first

If you only do one thing, paste a short brief at the start of the chat. It stops the assistant from guessing your system, tone and group:

TABLE BRIEF FOR THIS SESSION
- Role: [GM / player-prepping-a-character / fiction writer]
- System or format: [SYSTEM + EDITION / "system-agnostic" / novel / short story]
- Setting and tone: [e.g. "low-magic coastal frontier, tense and a little funny"]
- Group: [NUMBER of players, experience level, session length]
- Lines and veils: [topics to avoid entirely / handle off-screen]
- Things my group loves: [2-3 examples, e.g. "heists, found family, big reveals"]

HOW I'D LIKE YOU TO HELP
- Keep every idea original. Do not reuse named characters, places, plots or text from published games, books, films or shows.
- Make everything usable at a table: short, scannable, with names and one-line hooks.
- Offer options and let me choose. Don't railroad. Give me 3 choices when I ask for an idea.
- If I haven't named a system, stay system-agnostic. Never invent rules, stat blocks or numbers as if they were official. Label any number as a suggestion I must check against my rulebook.
- Respect my lines and veils.
- Ask up to 3 clarifying questions only if the answer would change your output a lot.

Reply "Ready" and wait for my first request.

Without it, you get generic fantasy. With it, you get answers that fit your table.

1. Build a world from one premise

Huge worlds are hard to run. A single strong "what if" is easier, because everything follows from it.

My world's single big premise: [WHAT IF..., e.g. "the sea gives up one lost ship every year"].
Give me a one-page world sketch: how this premise changes daily life, economy, travel, law and belief; who benefits and who suffers; and 5 second-order consequences most people wouldn't think of.
Then list 10 questions about the world I should answer next, ordered by how much they affect play. Don't use names or ideas from existing published settings.

The "second-order consequences" line is the useful part. It pushes the AI past the obvious (the sea gives up a ship, so salvage matters) to the interesting (salvage becomes a legal industry, and insurance fraud follows).

2. Make an NPC in thirty seconds

The most common emergency in any game is a player asking a stranger a question you didn't plan for.

Create an NPC for [SITUATION, e.g. "a night-shift ferry operator at a small river crossing"] in a [SETTING] game.
Give me: name, how they look in one detail, how they talk (a speech tic or phrase), what they want right now, what they're afraid of, one thing they know that the party would love to know, and what they'd want in exchange. Make the name fit the culture: [CULTURE / REGION].
Keep it to 80 words so I can read it in 30 seconds.

Keep it under 80 words. If you can't read an NPC in half a minute, you can't use it live.

3. Write a villain who believes they're right

Design an antagonist for a campaign about [PREMISE]. They should believe they're right.
Give: their goal, why it feels justified to them, what they've sacrificed, what they're willing to do and not do, what would make them stop, how they treat underlings, their public face versus their private face, and 3 ways the players might encounter them before a final confrontation. Avoid clichés (no secret royal family, no dead wife).
Then write one scene where the players hear this villain's argument and are tempted to agree.

The scene at the end is worth the whole prompt. If your players are tempted to agree with the villain for a moment, you've done your job.

4. Build a mystery that can't be derailed

Mysteries fail when players miss one clue. The three-clue rule fixes it: every conclusion has three different ways to reach it.

Design a mystery for [SETTING]. The central question is [WHO / WHAT / WHY]. Use the three-clue rule: for every key conclusion, include at least three different clues pointing to it.
Give: the truth (what really happened, in order), the suspects with motives, a clue list (each clue: where it's found, what it points to, and what happens if players miss it), 3 red herrings and a fallback if players get stuck.
Present the clue web as a table so I can check coverage.

5. Prep a session in ten minutes

When the players wander, you don't need a full adventure, just five beats.

The party is in [PLACE] and has just decided to [UNEXPECTED ACTION]. Create a self-contained side quest in 5 beats: a hook, a complication, a choice, a consequence and an aftermath.
It should use at least one NPC I already have: [NPC], link back to the main plot by one thread, and be playable in 30 to 45 minutes. Give me 3 improvisation lines for NPCs and a list of 3 things that could go wrong.

Habits that make AI prep better

  • Keep it original. Ask for original names and ideas, and don't paste published adventure text into an AI tool to rewrite it. Genres and tropes are free for everyone, but specific characters, settings and text belong to their creators.
  • Check every number. AI can invent rules or misquote stat blocks. Treat any number as a suggestion and look it up in your own rulebook.
  • Ask for options. "Give me 3" beats "give me one", and you stay the author.
  • Read lines and veils. Agree on topics to avoid with your players, and add them to your brief so the AI respects them too.
  • Edit. The best prep is the AI's draft plus your voice.

Writers can use these too

Prompts 06 (premise to world), 17 (villain) and the culture and timeline prompts in the full pack work for fiction as they are. A world that holds together at the table also holds together on the page.
